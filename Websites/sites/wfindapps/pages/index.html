WCopyfind

Compares documents with each other and shows you every phrase they share. Teachers use it to catch plagiarism and copying between students; researchers use it to trace how text moves between sources.

  • Checks hundreds or thousands of documents in one run
  • Finds copying even when a few words were changed
  • Shows each matching pair side by side, with the shared passages highlighted

WRepeatfind

Looks inside one document, or a whole book, for phrases it repeats. Writers and editors use it to catch the paragraph that was moved but never deleted, and the sentence that turns up twice.

  • Checks articles, essays, reports, and multi-chapter books
  • Finds repeats even when they were lightly edited
  • Highlights every copy of each repeat, and lets you dismiss intentional ones

Why people use them

🔒

Private by design

Everything happens on your own computer. No uploads, no cloud, no account, and no copy of your documents kept anywhere.

⚡

Fast

A clever sorting approach lets WCopyfind compare hundreds of documents, and WRepeatfind check a whole book, in seconds.

🎁

Free and open

Both programs are free for everyone and open source under the GNU General Public License. The code is on GitHub.

📄

Reads your files

Word documents (.docx and .doc), plain text, web pages, and PDFs that contain text. Any language that puts spaces between words works.

🖍️

Clear results

Reports open in your web browser, with matching passages highlighted, so you can see exactly what was copied or repeated.

🧰

Nothing to install

Each program is a single file. Unzip it and run it; delete it when you're done.

Who uses them

  • Teachers and professors checking term papers, lab reports, and take-home exams
  • Honor and conduct officers investigating suspected plagiarism
  • Researchers studying how language moves between news stories, legislation, lobbying documents, and scholarship
  • Writers, journalists, and editors checking their own work for accidental repetition
  • Students making sure their drafts don't repeat themselves, or a source, by mistake

Where they came from

WCopyfind grew out of software that Lou Bloomfield, a physics professor at the University of Virginia, wrote in 2001 to check the term papers in his large introductory physics course. He has shared it freely ever since, and it has been used by teachers and researchers around the world for more than twenty years.

In 2026 it was modernized, and WRepeatfind joined it, answering a request readers had been making for years: a way to find the repetition inside a document.
